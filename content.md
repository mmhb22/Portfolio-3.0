## Clearlight
accent: #7c6af7
tag: UX Research · Product Design · 2026
subtitle: A redesigned experience that allows users to stay in control of their saunas from mobile.
year: 2026
role: UX/UI Designer
tools: Figma · Android studio 
duration: —
image: images/projects/clearlight-banner.jpg

### Overview

Clearlight is a wellness company focused on infrared saunas, red light therapy, and cold plunge products for home and commercial use. Their products support recovery, relaxation, and everyday wellness through heat, light, and cold therapy experiences. As the solo designer, I owned the end-to-end redesign of the app experience.

- **Synthesized** Pain points from public app reviews
- **Reviewed** Early prototypes against real sauna behavior
- **Refined** Onboarding, controls, and status feedback 
- **Improved** Visual hierarchy across key app flows

![Clearlight app — overview](images/projects/clearlight-1.jpg)

### The Problem

Users couldn't tell what their sauna app was actually doing. Was it connected? Did that button press register? Was the session starting, saved, or stuck?
Without clear feedback at every step, people lost trust in the app — leading to high drop-off, a flood of support calls, and users unable to reliably control their own sauna sessions.



### Research

I reviewed public App Store and Google Play reviews for Clearlight and comparable connected sauna apps to identify patterns around setup, connectivity, controls, and user trust.


- **Connection clarity** Users needed clearer connection and device status
- **Guided setup** Setup flows needed better step-by-step direction
- **Trust** Connectivity issues reduced confidence in the app
- **Confirmation** Users wanted clearer feedback when actions were saved or activated
- **Remote control** Remote experiences needed more visible system feedback

I led the review of early prototypes against real sauna behavior — testing how design, app build, and hardware response held up together.

**Key insight** The app needed to do more than provide controls. It needed to guide users, confirm actions, and clearly communicate what the physical product was doing.


![Clearlight app — research findings](images/projects/clearlight-2.jpg)

### Solution

The design direction focused on making the app easier to follow from setup through daily use for both home and commercial users.

Commercial users often managed multiple saunas across locations, so a clear system for organizing devices was essential.


- **Guided onboarding** Clearer setup steps and progress feedback
- **Status feedback** Visible connection, loading, active, and error states
- **Action validation** Clear confirmation when actions are saved, active, or complete
- **Multi-device capability** A clear way for users to manage and control multiple saunas.

![Clearlight app — solution screens](images/projects/clearlight-3.jpg)

### Results

The app is still in active development, so results here are measured in design outcomes rather than shipped metrics.

- **Setup clarity** Redesigned onboarding and daily-use flows
- **Status feedback** Strengthened connection and device-state communication
- **Validation** Added clearer loading, saved, and complete states
- **Predictability** Made buttons and modals consistent across the app


![Clearlight app — final design outcomes](images/projects/clearlight-4.jpg)

## Patreon
accent: #f96b4b
tag: UX Research · UI Design· Interaction Design · 2024
subtitle: Redesigning Patreon’s Explore experience for creators and communities.
year: 2024
role: UX Researcher & Designer
tools: Figma · FigJam · Dovetail
duration: 10 weeks
image: images/projects/patreon-banner.jpg

### Overview

*This is a concept project completed as part of my UX bootcamp at Memorisely.*

Patreon is a membership platform that lets fans support creators through exclusive content, subscriptions, and community access.

This case study covers my redesign of Patreon's Explore experience; the space meant to help fans discover creators and projects across the platform.
The existing experience wasn't working for either side: users couldn't easily find creators aligned with their interests, and creators were getting lost in an increasingly crowded platform.


![Patreon Explore — existing experience audit and initial discovery](images/projects/patreon-1.jpg)

- **Scope** Explore experience — search, filtering, and creator discovery flow
- **Role** Research synthesis, user flows, filtering logic, interaction design, and high-fidelity UI
- **Goal** Make discovery more intentional for users and visibility more equitable for creators

### The Problem

Patreon has a large, diverse creator base, but Explore gave users no real way to navigate it. The core problem was a lack of guided discovery: people browsed by topic or interest, not by creator name, and the experience didn't reflect that. Creators, meanwhile, were nearly invisible unless a user already knew who they were looking for.


- **Visibility gap** Creators were difficult to find unless users already knew their name
- **Filtering** Limited filtering options caused early drop-off during casual browsing
- **Discovery intent** Users explored by topic or interest, not by creator name — the experience didn't reflect this
- **Trust barrier** Users subscribed more readily when content felt exclusive, consistent, and personally meaningful


### Research

We began with evaluative research — auditing Patreon's existing navigation and Explore experience, then benchmarking discovery models against direct competitors: YouTube, Kickstarter, Buy Me a Coffee, and Twitch. This surfaced early friction points, which we validated through six interviews with active and passive Patreon users.

Interview findings were organized through an affinity map to surface repeated themes. Discovery, we found, is not only about finding content — it is about trust, relevance, and connection.

![Affinity map — interview themes around creator discovery, filtering, and subscription intent](images/projects/patreon-2.jpg)

- **Interviews** 6 active and passive Patreon users
- **Competitive audit** YouTube, Kickstarter, Buy Me a Coffee, and Twitch — platforms competing for the same creator-supporter relationship
- **Key insight** Users wanted discovery to feel personal, not generic — they were more willing to explore (and pay) when content felt authentically connected to a creator's mission, not just algorithmically surfaced

### Solution

We used an Eisenhower prioritisation matrix to prioritize solutions by impact and effort, focusing on improvements directly tied to research findings. The goal was for Explore to feel more useful without overwhelming users or breaking Patreon's existing visual language.

The redesigned Explore section flow guides users from broad browsing to specific discovery — searching for creators or topics, filtering by content type or interest, browsing curated sections, and previewing creator cards before committing.


![Redesigned Explore flow — filters, curated sections, and creator cards](images/projects/patreon-3.jpg)

- **Filtering** Added filters by genre, region, and topic — letting users narrow discovery by what they actually care about rather than scrolling through unrelated content
- **Curated sections** "New This Week" and "Spotlight" cards surfaced creators beyond name recognition
- **+18 CTA** Designed a clear, easy-to-see opt-in for mature content — visible and accessible without being intrusive, so users with those preferences aren't left guessing
- **Creator cards** Preview-first approach let users assess fit before clicking through

### Results

The redesigned Explore concept delivered a more structured, intentional discovery experience while staying within Patreon's existing UI language.

| More intentional | Better visibility | Flexible discovery |
|-----------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Guided browsing by interest, topic, and content type | Curated sections surface creators beyond search | The redesigned flow works equally well for active searching and casual browsing — meeting users wherever their discovery intent starts |

## Slack
accent: #2e7d5e
tag: UX Design · Mobile · Collaboration · 2023
subtitle: Making informal team conversations easier to notice, plan, and join.
year: 2023
role: UX & UI Designer
tools: Figma · FigJam · Google Drive · Dovetail
methods: Design Thinking · Usability Heuristics · Surveys · Benchmarking · SWOT · Affinity Map · Mind Map · IA Map · Crazy 4s · User Flow · Wireframing · Prototyping
duration: 4 weeks
image: images/projects/slack-banner.jpg

### Overview

*This is a concept project completed as part of my UX bootcamp at Memorisely.*

Slack Huddle is a quick audio or video conversation feature designed for informal team communication — helping teams jump into lightweight conversations without scheduling a full meeting.
For this case study, I collaborated with another designer to improve the Huddle experience, focusing on discoverability, coordination, and missed huddles. The goal was to make Huddles easier to find, easier to plan, and easier to respond to while staying within Slack's familiar communication patterns.


![Slack Huddle redesign — discovery and scheduling flow](images/projects/slack-1.jpg)

- **Scope** Huddle discoverability, scheduling flow, mobile interaction patterns
- **Team** Two UX designers
- **Goal** Reduce missed huddles and lower the coordination effort around informal conversations

### The Problem

Huddles are meant to support spontaneous conversation, but users frequently missed them or struggled to coordinate them at the right time. The same informality that makes Huddles useful also makes them easy to overlook — especially when users are in another meeting, away from Slack, or moving between tasks.

All huddle activity was treated equally regardless of urgency, and there was no lightweight way to plan one ahead of time without resorting to a formal calendar invite.

- **Visibility gap** Active huddles were easy to miss when users were focused elsewhere
- **Coordination overhead** Scheduling a huddle required manual back-and-forth in chat
- **Mobile friction** Quick responses and joins were not optimised for on-the-go use
- **Feature feel** Any scheduling solution risked making Huddles feel like a formal meeting tool

### Research

We researched how users currently notice Slack activity, join conversations, and manage their workday across messages, channels, and meetings. Research combined user interviews with observation of existing Slack behaviour patterns.

The main insight was that users needed a better way to coordinate huddles without turning them into formal meetings. Users found Huddles useful precisely because they felt casual — any solution had to preserve that quality.

![Affinity map — Huddle usage patterns, feedback themes, engagement levels, scheduling behaviours, and pain points](images/projects/slack-2.jpg)

- **How users discover active huddles** and why they miss them
- **Coordination patterns** around informal conversations
- **Mobile navigation habits** within Slack
- **Key insight** Huddles should live where users already look — unread messages, active conversation areas — not in a separate scheduling tool

### Solution

The design direction focused on making huddles more visible and easier to coordinate while staying close to existing Slack behaviours. Rather than building a heavy scheduling system, we designed a lightweight huddle planning flow that fits naturally into Slack's communication model.

The flow let users see active or upcoming huddles, schedule a lightweight huddle, and respond quickly from mobile — using familiar patterns like unread message indicators and swipe interactions so the feature felt like a natural extension of what already existed.

![Wireframes — calendar view, huddle creation flow, attendee selection, and invite screen](images/projects/slack-3.jpg)

- **Huddle status system**
  Clear visual states for active, upcoming, missed, and suggested huddles
- **Lightweight scheduling**
  Plan a huddle without leaving the conversation context
- **Mobile-first interactions**
  Swipe gestures and placement near familiar Slack activity patterns
- **Familiar anchoring**
  Feature surfaced in unread messages and active conversation areas, not a separate tab

### Results

The redesign improved visibility for active and upcoming huddles, reduced manual coordination around quick conversations, and established a lightweight scheduling model that preserved the informal feel of Huddles.


| Improved visibility | Reduced coordination | Mobile-first |
|---------------------|---------------------|--------------|
| Active, missed & upcoming huddle states | Lightweight scheduling without calendar overhead | Swipe interactions and familiar Slack patterns |

tag: Publication Design · Product Manual · 2026
subtitle: Booklet design and custom assembly illustrations for Clearlight's wellness product line.
year: 2026
role: Publication Designer · Illustrator
tools: InDesign · Illustrator · Figma
duration: 3 weeks
image: images/projects/Manual/CL_Banner_Manual.png

### Overview

Clearlight produces a range of wellness products that ship with assembly and setup instructions. The existing documentation was text-heavy and difficult to follow — the goal was to redesign it as a clear, well-structured booklet that customers could actually use during unboxing.

The work involved two main areas: reformatting and typesetting dense technical copy into a readable booklet layout, and creating custom step-by-step illustrations showing how to assemble each product.

### Design

The booklet layout follows a spread-based structure — each step gets enough space to breathe, with illustrations and copy working together rather than competing. Typography and hierarchy were designed to guide a reader who is assembling something with their hands, not sitting at a desk.

The illustrations were drawn in Illustrator to be clear and consistent across different product types — showing parts, orientation, and order of assembly without relying on photography.
